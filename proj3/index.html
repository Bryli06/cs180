Part A · Inputs and settings

I used the course’s pretrained PixNerd-XXL-P16-T2I model without training. All inputs are RGB images at 512 × 512, normalized to \([-1,1]\). The cat and pond photographs are center-cropped to squares; the color portrait was already square. I centered the tall monochrome drawing on a white square before resizing so the complete composition remains visible.

The cat is the shared input for Sections 1.1–1.5. The cat and pond are my two selected test images for editing; the pond also serves as the web-image example. The Campanile is supplied by the course.

I used Euler step size \(h=0.02\) and float32 model evaluation and integration. The initial Colab runs used an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB; the waterfall and skull/rose refinements used an A100-SXM4-40GB. Denoising and editing use fixed seed 180. Generation comparisons use seeds 180–184. Unless stated otherwise, the condition is “a high quality photo.” Guidance uses an empty-string unconditional prompt and \(w=7\).

1.1 Noisy training inputs

For clean image \(x_1\) and one fixed Gaussian noise tensor \(\epsilon\), I construct

\[x_t=(1-t)\epsilon+t x_1.\qquad t\in[0,1]\]

Time runs from pure noise at \(t=0\) to the clean input at \(t=1\). I use fixed seed 180 and reuse the same noise tensor at all three times.

At t = 0.25, the broad white shape of the cat is still visible, but the fur and table edges are heavily obscured. Increasing t reveals the ears, fur texture, and furniture.

1.2 Gaussian denoising

The mixture scales the signal by \(t\), so I first divide by \(t\) and then apply a Gaussian blur: \(\hat x_1=G_\sigma*(x_t/t)\). I tried \(\sigma\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}\), using kernel width \(6\sigma+1\), and selected the lowest mean-squared error against the known clean input separately at each time.

The selected blur widths decrease from σ = 8 to 4 to 2 as the input gets cleaner. At t = 0.25, large colored blotches remain and the fur becomes a smooth mass. At t = 0.75, the cat is recognizable, but grain remains and individual fur strands are softened.

1.3 One-step denoising

The model predicts a velocity \(v_\theta(x_t,t,c)\). One prediction estimates the displacement from the current time to the endpoint:

\[\hat x_1=x_t+(1-t)v_\theta(x_t,t,c).\]

I use the generic photo prompt with no classifier-free guidance in this section. The same saved inputs and original noise are reused throughout the denoising comparison.

The model removes the colored grain much more effectively than the Gaussian baseline. At t = 0.25, it reconstructs the cat and table but smooths away much of the fur and the lamp in the background. At t = 0.75, the estimate preserves substantially more fur detail and the original outline.

1.4 Visualizing velocity

The predicted and ground-truth updates are:

\[\Delta_{\mathrm{pred}}=(1-t)v_\theta(x_t,t,c),\qquad \Delta_{\mathrm{gt}}=(1-t)(x_1-\epsilon)=x_1-x_t.\]

The error map is the root-mean-square difference across the three color channels at each pixel, computed before clipping or display scaling:

\[E_t(i,j)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k\in\{R,G,B\}}\big[\Delta_{\mathrm{pred}}(i,j,k)-\Delta_{\mathrm{gt}}(i,j,k)\big]^2}.\]

All signed RGB updates use the same symmetric range \([-3.971,3.971]\), with zero shown as middle gray. Every error map uses the same linear scale, from 0.000 to 0.946.

0 · low RMS error0.946 · high

The two update images look similar because both contain a large noise-canceling component. Their difference is concentrated along the fur, ears, and sharp table edges; the smooth wall has much less error. Mean pixel RMS falls from 0.0623 to 0.0373 to 0.0234 across the three times. This decrease reflects both a cleaner input and the smaller remaining-time factor (1 − t), so it is not a direct comparison of unscaled velocity error.

1.5 Euler denoising

Euler integration predicts again after each small movement:

\[x_{t+h}=x_t+h\,v_\theta(x_t,t,c).\]

Starting from the saved \(t=0.50\) cat mixture, I take 25 updates of size 0.02. The figures include the initial state, every fifth update, and the final state. The sampler also shortens its last step when a starting time does not divide the remaining interval evenly.

Its final image has more distinct fur texture than the one-step estimate from t = 0.50, but that texture differs from the original. 1.6 Generating images from noise

I start Euler integration at \(t=0\) from five independent Gaussian noise tensors and take 50 updates. All five samples use the same broad prompt; their seeds are 180–184.

Prompt: “a high quality photo”

All five samples are portraits despite the generic prompt. Seed 180 has almost no facial features, seed 181 has distorted torso anatomy, and seed 183 has rough, inconsistent facial texture. Seeds 182 and 184 are more coherent.

2.1 Classifier-free guidance

At the same state and time, I evaluate both the requested prompt and the empty prompt, then amplify their difference:

\[v_{\mathrm{CFG}}=v_c+w(v_c-v_u),\qquad w=7.\]

Under this convention, \(w=0\) reproduces the ordinary conditional prediction. Each pair reuses the starting noise from Section 1.6, using seeds 180–184.

Across these five pairs, guidance improves eyes, mouths, hair, and lighting, especially for seeds 180 and 183. It also changes the depicted person. Both sets favor portraits, but the guided outputs are more similar centered headshots with smooth backgrounds, reducing compositional variety.

3.1 Image-to-image editing

For each image, I interpolate it with noise at the selected starting time and integrate to \(t=1\) using the guided velocity. The seven starting times are \(0.02,0.04,0.06,0.10,0.14,0.25,0.50\). A smaller starting time leaves less input information and gives the model more freedom to alter the scene. Every sweep uses seed 180, \(h=0.02\), and \(w=7\).

Prompt: “a high quality photo”

Campanile

At the lowest starting times, the tower becomes a smooth white cylinder or tapered pole against a blue sky. A dark window appears around t = 0.10–0.14, the belfry returns at t = 0.25, and t = 0.50 recovers much of the clock and masonry.

Cat photograph

At t = 0.02, the model produces a laptop scene; at t = 0.04–0.06, the pale curled shape becomes bedding. Around t = 0.10–0.14, white fabric and hand-like forms occupy the original cat’s position. A recognizable but very smooth cat returns at t = 0.25, and the fur becomes convincing at t = 0.50. The room’s neutral colors and horizontal support survive even when the subject changes completely.

Shirogane Blue Pond

The smallest starting times reinterpret the red and turquoise bands as a monitor or a sign. At t = 0.06, the scene is mostly soft horizontal color; t = 0.10–0.14 recovers blurred tree silhouettes. Fine trunks and reflections return at t = 0.25–0.50.

Hand-drawn and web images

The credited Shirogane Blue Pond photograph is my web image as well as my second test image; its full seven-time sweep is shown directly above. I apply the same generic-photo procedure to the two supplied drawings below. The monochrome drawing’s white margins preserve its full tall composition.

Monochrome figure drawing

A seated human figure begins to emerge at t = 0.14; t = 0.25 returns to the original arrangement with smoother shading, and t = 0.50 preserves more of the drawn lines. The low-time results look more photographic but lose the figures.

Larger starting times increasingly preserve the original stylized face and soft painted surfaces.

3.3 Visual anagrams

Let \(R\) rotate an image by 180°. At every step, I predict one guided velocity in the upright view and another in the rotated view. I rotate the second prediction back before averaging:

\[v=\frac{1}{2}\left[v_{\mathrm{CFG}}(x_t,t,p_1)+R\!\left(v_{\mathrm{CFG}}(R(x_t),t,p_2)\right)\right].\]

Both spatial axes are flipped by the rotation. I integrate this averaged field from noise with 50 Euler steps.

Old man / campfire

Upright prompt: “an oil painting of an old man”. Rotated prompt: “an oil painting of people around a campfire”. Seed 180.

The upright image reads as an old man lit by warm orange light. After rotation, the bright forehead becomes the campfire, while smaller faces near the beard become people gathered above it.

Mountain village / horse

Upright prompt: “a lithograph of a snowy mountain village”. Rotated prompt: “a lithograph of a horse”. Seed 181.

The upright view contains snowy roofs, fences, and mountain peaks. Rotating it reveals a horse with a distinct face and front legs. The separation is less convincing than in the first example: the horse’s upside-down face is still visible among the village roofs, and both views have very faint contrast.

3.4 Hybrid images

I combine low frequencies of one guided velocity with high frequencies of a second guided velocity, then use this composite field in every Euler update:

\[v=G_\sigma*v_{\mathrm{distant}}+\left(v_{\mathrm{close}}-G_\sigma*v_{\mathrm{close}}\right).\]

The Gaussian uses \(\sigma=16\) pixels and a 265 × 265 kernel at 512 × 512. Each example starts from noise and uses 50 Euler steps. The 64 × 64 preview is a downsampled view of the final image, approximating a more distant viewing scale.

At blur radii of 4 and 2 pixels, the skull dominated both sizes. I kept \(\sigma=16\) for both examples.

Skull / white roses

Distant prompt: “a black and white charcoal drawing of a human skull seen from the front, large hollow black eye sockets, centered on a plain black background”. Close prompt: “a black and white charcoal drawing of a dense bouquet of white roses, intricate small petals and dark leaves, centered on a plain black background”. Seed 180.

At full size, individual white roses, curled petals, and dark leaves are distinct. In the small preview, the flowers merge into the skull’s outline, eye sockets, and nose. The skull arrangement remains visible up close, so the effect is a partial hybrid.

Lion / sunflowers

Distant prompt: “an oil painting of a lion's face”. Close prompt: “an oil painting of a bouquet of sunflowers”. Seed 186.

The large image emphasizes individual sunflower petals and the central bouquet. In the small preview, the two dark upper flower centers and warm surrounding shape suggest a lion’s eyes and mane, while petals occupy the muzzle. The flowers remain prominent at both scales, so the distant lion interpretation is suggestive rather than unambiguous.

Part A · What I learned

It was really interesting to see how classifier-free guidance works and how much it changed the generated images. I learned how the starting noise level controls how much of the original image stays in an edit. I was surprised that the hybrid images could show different subjects at different viewing distances, even though the effect wasn’t always clear. The hybrids and rotating anagrams helped me understand image geometry better, especially how the same shapes and details can fit together to look like different things.

Part B · Flow matching from scratch

For these experiments, I train on the 60,000 MNIST training images and reserve the 10,000 test images for denoising examples. Each image is a single 28 × 28 grayscale channel with values in \([0,1]\). No pretrained weights are used in Part B. Training images are shuffled, and fresh Gaussian noise is drawn when each batch is processed.

The architecture follows the course’s UNet diagrams and starter interfaces. I use fixed seed 180 for initialization and sampling.

1.1 The UNet

The encoder reduces the spatial resolution twice, from 28 × 28 to 14 × 14 and then 7 × 7. Average pooling compresses the last feature map to 1 × 1. The decoder expands it again and concatenates matching encoder features along the channel dimension, preserving local information through the skip connections.

The convolution blocks use batch normalization and GELU activations. Downsampling uses stride-two convolutions; upsampling uses transposed convolutions. A final convolution returns one output channel. The hidden width is \(D=128\) for the single-step denoisers and \(D=64\) for both flow models.

1.2 Noising MNIST

For a clean digit \(x\), I add Gaussian noise without reducing the clean signal:

\[z=x+\sigma\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).\]

I hold the image and sampled noise fixed while varying \(\sigma\). The noisy tensors remain unclipped for the model; only the displayed images are clipped to \([0,1]\).

With the noise pattern fixed, increasing σ makes bright noise pixels more prominent around the 7’s strokes. The digit is easy to recognize at low noise levels and much harder to distinguish at σ = 1.0.

1.2.1 Training the denoiser

The single-step network predicts the clean image directly and minimizes mean squared error:

\[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{denoise}}=\mathbb E_{x,\epsilon}\!\left[\|D_\theta(x+0.5\epsilon)-x\|_2^2\right].\]

I train for five epochs with hidden width 128, batch size 256, and Adam at a constant learning rate of \(10^{-4}\). The epoch-one and epoch-five comparisons use the same test digits and the same noise at \(\sigma=0.5\).

Most digits are already recognizable after one epoch. By epoch five, the reconstructions have cleaner backgrounds and more consistent stroke contours, although some strokes differ from the clean targets. The loss drops sharply at the beginning and then improves more gradually.

1.2.2 Other noise levels

The denoiser was trained only at \(\sigma=0.5\). I keep the test digits and underlying noise fixed, vary its amplitude, and evaluate the same epoch-five checkpoint without further training.

For these test digits, σ = 0.0–0.6 mostly preserves readable shapes, although even the clean inputs are slightly smoothed. At σ = 0.8, extra strokes and residual background spots appear; the 1 in the third column becomes closer to a 7. At σ = 1.0, several contours are distorted or ambiguous.

1.2.3 Denoising pure noise

I train a separate copy of the same width-128 network for five epochs with batch size 256 and Adam at \(10^{-4}\). Its input is now independent Gaussian noise, paired with a randomly encountered training digit as the target. The sample figures use 40 fixed noise inputs at both checkpoints.

Different noise inputs produce nearly identical blurred ovals with overlapping loops, closely resembling the mean training image. The epoch-five outputs are smoother than those from epoch one, but still lack distinct, varied digits. The loss quickly plateaus.

For squared error, the best population prediction is the conditional mean. Here the input noise is independent of the target digit, so that mean is the same for every input:

\[D^*(z)=\mathbb E[x\mid z]=\mathbb E[x]=\mu.\]
\[\mathbb E\|a-x\|_2^2=\|a-\mu\|_2^2+\mathbb E\|x-\mu\|_2^2.\]

The first term is minimized by predicting the dataset’s pixelwise mean for every input.

2.1 Time conditioning

The flow network receives both an image and a time \(t\in[0,1]\). Two fully connected blocks, each with two linear layers and a GELU between them, map this scalar to channel vectors of sizes \(2D\) and \(D\). These vectors multiply the unflattened feature map and the output of the first up block, broadcasting over spatial dimensions.

\[h_{\mathrm{unflatten}}\leftarrow h_{\mathrm{unflatten}}\odot T_1(t),\qquad h_{\mathrm{up1}}\leftarrow h_{\mathrm{up1}}\odot T_2(t).\]

The output is a velocity field with the same shape as the image.

2.2 Flow-matching training

For each training example, I independently sample Gaussian noise \(x_0\) and a uniform time \(t\), form a point on the straight path to the clean digit \(x_1\), and regress the path’s velocity:

\[x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1,\qquad u_{\mathrm{target}}=x_1-x_0.\]
\[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{flow}}=\mathbb E\!\left[\|u_\theta(x_t,t)-(x_1-x_0)\|_2^2\right].\]

I train the width-64 network for ten epochs using batch size 64 and Adam. The learning rate starts at \(10^{-2}\) and is multiplied by \(0.1^{1/10}\) after each epoch. Noise and time are resampled for each batch.

After a large early spike, the flow loss drops quickly and then decreases gradually.

2.3 Time-conditioned samples

Starting from Gaussian noise, I integrate the learned velocity from \(t=0\) to \(t=1\) with 50 Euler steps:

\[x_{k+1}=x_k+\frac{1}{50}u_\theta\!\left(x_k,\frac{k}{50}\right),\qquad k=0,\ldots,49.\]

The 40 starting noise tensors are held fixed across the epoch-one, epoch-five, and epoch-ten checkpoints. No digit label is supplied.

Each grid position uses the same starting noise. Epoch one has many fragmented or unreadable shapes; by epoch five, most have recognizable loops and connected strokes. Epoch ten refines them further, but several remain ambiguous, including the top-left sample. Most of the visible improvement occurs between epochs one and five.

2.4 Class conditioning

I represent the requested digit as a ten-dimensional one-hot vector. Two additional fully connected blocks embed the class at the same decoder locations as the time embeddings. Class features scale the activations, while time features shift them:

\[h_{\mathrm{unflatten}}\leftarrow C_1(c)\odot h_{\mathrm{unflatten}}+T_1(t),\qquad h_{\mathrm{up1}}\leftarrow C_2(c)\odot h_{\mathrm{up1}}+T_2(t).\]

During training, I independently replace 10% of the one-hot vectors with an all-zero vector. This trains an unconditional branch; the zero vector is distinct from the one-hot vector for digit zero. During sampling, both predictions use the same image and time, and I combine them as

\[u_{\mathrm{CFG}}=u_{\mathrm{uncond}}+\gamma\left(u_{\mathrm{cond}}-u_{\mathrm{uncond}}\right),\qquad\gamma=5.\]

In this Part B convention, \(\gamma=1\) recovers the ordinary class-conditioned prediction.

2.5 Class-conditioned training

The class-conditioned model uses the same flow target, hidden width 64, batch size 64, and ten-epoch schedule as the time-only model. Adam starts at \(10^{-2}\), with an exponential multiplier of \(0.1^{1/10}\) after every epoch. Class dropout is applied per training example.

The loss drops rapidly after its initial spike, then flattens.

2.6 Class-conditioned samples

I generate four instances of each digit using 300 Euler steps and guidance scale \(\gamma=5\). Each row requests digits 0–9 in order, and the starting noise is held fixed across checkpoints.

Class labels already organize the epoch-one outputs into the requested columns, but several digits have broken or irregular strokes. By epochs five and ten, the four rows are much cleaner and the requested 0–9 sequence is readable. Class conditioning gives clearer control over digit identity in these grids; classifier accuracy was not measured.

Removing the learning-rate scheduler

I train a separate class-conditioned model from the same initialization, with the same data order and random seeds. I remove exponential decay and compensate by reducing Adam’s constant learning rate to \(10^{-3}\). Hidden width, batch size, ten-epoch training duration, class dropout, and sampling settings remain the same. This changes both the schedule and its initial learning rate; it is not a constant-\(10^{-2}\) ablation.

The lower constant learning rate produces readable class-conditioned samples even at epoch one. By epoch ten, both runs follow the requested digit columns with similar legibility and close training losses. The constant-rate outputs have some thinner strokes and a curled 1 in the last row; the scheduled run’s 1s are more uniform.

Part B · What I learned

I learned that for single-step denoisers, it only works when the input already contains the essence of a digit, but random noise just goes towards a combination of everything. To fix this, I learned how to do flow matching through class conditioning to make the requested class much clearer. It was super interesting to see how time only sampling can leave digits ambiguous.
