A.2 Recover homographies

A homography maps a source point \((x,y)\) to a reference point \((u,v)\):

\[ s\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}h_1&h_2&h_3\\h_4&h_5&h_6\\h_7&h_8&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Fixing \(h_{33}=1\) leaves eight unknowns. Each match contributes two equations to \(Ah=b\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x_i&y_i&1&0&0&0&-u_i x_i&-u_i y_i\\ 0&0&0&x_i&y_i&1&-v_i x_i&-v_i y_i \end{bmatrix}h =\begin{bmatrix}u_i\\v_i\end{bmatrix}. \]

I fit 12–16 manually selected matches per image pair with least squares. To improve numerical conditioning, I center each point set and scale its mean distance from the origin to \(\sqrt{2}\). After fitting the normalized matrix \(H_n\), I recover \(H=T_r^{-1}H_nT_s\), where \(T_s\) and \(T_r\) are the source and reference normalizations.

I selected corresponding landmarks by inspecting enlarged images: window corners, roof edges, ceiling fixtures, and door frames. I checked the point-fit errors and reinspected unclear selections. The rectification corners were also selected visually. In the figures below, matching numbers identify the same feature; each matrix maps the left image to the right.

A.3 Warp and rectify the images

For each output pixel at \((x,y)\), I compute \(H^{-1}[x,y,1]^T=[a,b,c]^T\). The corresponding source position is \((a/c,b/c)\), where I sample the image. This inverse mapping avoids holes between forward-mapped pixels.

Nearest neighbor rounds to the closest pixel. Bilinear sampling averages four neighbors. With \(x_0=\lfloor x\rfloor\), \(y_0=\lfloor y\rfloor\), \(a=x-x_0\), and \(b=y-y_0\),

\[\begin{aligned} I(x,y)={}&(1-a)(1-b)I(x_0,y_0)\\ &+a(1-b)I(x_0+1,y_0)\\ &+(1-a)bI(x_0,y_0+1)\\ &+abI(x_0+1,y_0+1). \end{aligned}\]

General warps use the transformed corners to size the canvas. Rectification uses the chosen target rectangle. A separate mask distinguishes empty space from black pixels.

Wall sign

The sign was photographed at a steeper angle. Rectification straightens it, but the enlarged text remains soft.

A.4 Blend the images into mosaics

I keep the reference image fixed and warp the other views into its coordinates with bilinear sampling. Their transformed bounds determine the shared canvas.

Each image receives weights that increase with distance from its edges. I warp the weighted colors and weights together, then divide their sums:

\[ M(p)=\frac{\sum_k\mathcal W_{H_k}(w_kI_k)(p)} {\sum_k\mathcal W_{H_k}(w_k)(p)}. \]

Here \(\mathcal W\) is inverse warping. Overlaps transition gradually between views, and uncovered pixels stay transparent. The figures retain the full warped boundaries.

Atrium

The balconies mostly align, but nearby railing bars appear doubled. This is consistent with a small camera-center shift; point-selection error may also contribute. Feathering softens the seam without fixing the misalignment.

Courtyard

Window rows and floor boundaries mostly join across the views. The wide planar projection stretches the sides, and some brightness differences remain near the glass.

Skyline

What I learned

It was super cool to learn how to make mosaics and understand the difficulties involved. The atrium’s doubled railings are visible even when the selected landmarks line up closely. Rectifying the sign also showed the trade-off between nearest neighbor and bilinear sampling: bilinear makes the text smoother, but it cannot restore detail missing from the original photo.
