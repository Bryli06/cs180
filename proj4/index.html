A.1 Shoot the pictures

I resized the photographs to 1050 × 1400 pixels before marking landmarks. All point coordinates and alignment errors use that resolution.

When the camera rotates about a fixed center, a single homography can align objects at different depths.

A.2 Recover homographies

A homography maps a source point \((x,y)\) to a reference point \((u,v)\):

\[ s\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}h_1&h_2&h_3\\h_4&h_5&h_6\\h_7&h_8&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Fixing \(h_{33}=1\) leaves eight unknowns. Each match contributes two equations to \(Ah=b\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x_i&y_i&1&0&0&0&-u_i x_i&-u_i y_i\\ 0&0&0&x_i&y_i&1&-v_i x_i&-v_i y_i \end{bmatrix}h =\begin{bmatrix}u_i\\v_i\end{bmatrix}. \]

I fit 12–16 manually selected matches per image pair with least squares. To improve numerical conditioning, I center each point set and scale its mean distance from the origin to \(\sqrt{2}\). After fitting the normalized matrix \(H_n\), I recover \(H=T_r^{-1}H_nT_s\), where \(T_s\) and \(T_r\) are the source and reference normalizations.

I marked window corners, roof edges, ceiling fixtures, and door frames on enlarged photos. Matching numbers identify the same landmark in both views. Each matrix maps the left photo into the right photo’s coordinates.

A.3 Warp and rectify the images

For each output pixel at \((x,y)\), I compute \(H^{-1}[x,y,1]^T=[a,b,c]^T\). The corresponding source position is \((a/c,b/c)\), where I sample the image. This inverse mapping avoids holes between forward-mapped pixels.

Nearest neighbor rounds to the closest pixel. Bilinear sampling averages four neighbors. With \(x_0=\lfloor x\rfloor\), \(y_0=\lfloor y\rfloor\), \(a=x-x_0\), and \(b=y-y_0\),

\[\begin{aligned} I(x,y)={}&(1-a)(1-b)I(x_0,y_0)\\ &+a(1-b)I(x_0+1,y_0)\\ &+(1-a)bI(x_0,y_0+1)\\ &+abI(x_0+1,y_0+1). \end{aligned}\]

For rectification I marked the four corners of each object and chose a rectangular output by eye. The proportions are approximate because I did not measure either object.

A.4 Blend the images into mosaics

I kept the reference photo fixed and used bilinear sampling to warp the other views onto a canvas large enough to hold their transformed corners.

To soften the seams, I gave pixels near the edge of each photo less weight. I warped the weighted colors and their weights together and divided the two sums.

\[ M(p)=\frac{\sum_k\mathcal W_{H_k}(w_kI_k)(p)} {\sum_k\mathcal W_{H_k}(w_k)(p)}. \]

Here \(\mathcal W\) denotes inverse warping. Pixels outside all the photos are transparent.

The balcony edges line up better than the bars of the nearby railing, which appear doubled. A slight shift in camera position or an imprecise landmark could account for the offset.

B.1 Detect and select corners

I ran a single-scale Harris detector on each grayscale photo. It picks out changes in brightness in two directions, such as the corner where a window frame meets a sill. I kept local peaks in a 7 × 7 neighborhood, at least 20 pixels from the image edge.

Many Harris corners landed on the atrium railings. To spread them out, I used adaptive non-maximal suppression (ANMS). I measured the distance from each corner to the nearest corner whose Harris response exceeded its own by more than one ninth. I kept the 800 corners with the largest distances.

B.2 Describe each corner

Around each corner I took a 40 × 40 patch from the blurred grayscale photo. Averaging each 5 × 5 block gave me an 8 × 8 descriptor. I subtracted the descriptor’s mean and divided by its standard deviation so the same edge pattern could match across changes in brightness and contrast.

B.3 Match features between views

A railing bar can look like several others in the next photo. I kept a match only if both descriptors were each other’s nearest neighbor and the closest reference descriptor was less than 0.8 times as far away as the second closest. I used Euclidean distance between the 8 × 8 patches.

B.4 Fit homographies and compare mosaics

For each RANSAC trial I drew four matches and fit a homography with the Part A solver, skipping samples with three nearly collinear points in either view. I counted a match as an inlier if its projected point landed within four pixels of its partner. I refit the best-supported homography on its inliers and stitched the mosaic with the Part A blend.

What I learned

The railing still looks doubled where the atrium photos overlap. I checked the automatic fit against landmarks I had picked by hand. In the right atrium view, the automatic inliers had a median error of 1.19 pixels. On my hand-picked landmarks the same transform missed by a median of 4.86 pixels. For the courtyard and skyline pairs, the medians on hand-picked points were 1.43–1.65 pixels.
